About

Hi, I'm Martin.

I'm not a software engineer by training, and I didn't take anything like a traditional route into technology. Most of what's on this site, I taught myself — recently, and out of necessity as much as curiosity.

Why Bronks Labs exists

I build things because I want to understand them, not to impress anyone with the result. Every project here starts with a real question — sometimes the answer turns into a working server, sometimes a better workflow, sometimes just finally understanding why an idea didn't work the way I expected. The finished thing is nice to have. The understanding I got building it is the actual point.

Why I document everything

I'll be honest — documenting doesn't come naturally to me. Left to my own instincts, I'd rather just build the next thing and move on. But it's the actual reason this site and my GitHub exist, so I make myself do it anyway, on purpose, even when it feels like a chore. The mistakes, the redesigns, the things that didn't work the first time — those turn out to be more useful, to me later and occasionally to someone else, than a polished write-up of just the finished version would be.

Beyond the technology

Technology isn't the only thing I build. I write fantasy, I like world-building, and I'm genuinely interested in how AI can support that process without taking it over — I'm about equally happy debugging a Docker container as I am rewriting a chapter that isn't working yet. Outside of all of it, I'm just someone who likes understanding how things work — hardware, software, networks, or a story that hasn't come together yet. Curiosity is usually where the next thing starts.

Looking ahead

Bronks Labs isn't a finished destination — it's an ongoing record of what I'm learning, what I'm building, what breaks, and what's next. There will always be another project, another mistake worth learning from, and another page to write about it. That's exactly how I want it to stay.

How it started

I started working at eighteen, as a security guard, and later moved into warehousing and logistics after relocating to the UK at twenty-five. That work taught me things that turned out to matter more than I expected at the time — reliability, organisation, the discipline of solving practical problems under pressure. I didn't know it yet, but those habits would end up shaping how I approach everything I build now.

About three years after moving, I got seriously ill. What followed was close to fifteen years of my life built around it — the last five of those spent in and out of dialysis and surgery — before I finally received a transplant. I'm not going to lay out the details here; it's not really what this page is for. But it's the honest answer to "why did this start so late," and I'd rather say that plainly than leave the gap unexplained.

What came after the transplant was time and space I hadn't had in years — room to slow down and actually learn something for its own sake. What began as curiosity about artificial intelligence turned into a genuine pull toward understanding how modern technology actually works, underneath the surface. One question led to another, and before long I was rebuilding old computers, learning Linux, running servers, and documenting all of it as I went.

I didn't set out to build a homelab. I just kept following whatever question came next.
